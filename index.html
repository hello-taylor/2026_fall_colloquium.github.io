    Dr. Jack Kaye

    Former Associate Director for Research, Earth Science Division, NASA Science Mission Directorate

    • Remote sensing
    • Earth system science
    • Atmospheric chemistry
    • Research leadership

    Looking at the Earth from Space: From Photons to Products for Science and Society

    The vantage point of space provides a unique opportunity for looking down at the Earth to characterize its component sub-systems (atmosphere, ocean, cryosphere, biosphere, land surface and interior), understand how they behave both individually and interacting with each other and with the natural and human-induced drivers of Earth System change, contributing to the prediction of future Earth system evolution, and the use of the resulting knowledge to help us understand how to live better on our home planet — both now and in the future.

    This talk will provide an overview of space-based remote sensing of Earth, drawing extensively on examples from NASA’s Earth Science program, where I worked for over 40 years. It will also emphasize the importance of integrating across vantage points (space, air, surface), approaches (especially observations and models), programs, and communities, and demonstrate the synergies that come from capturing the synergies between science, technology, and society.

    Biography

    Jack Kaye retired on April 30, 2025 after a 41-year career at NASA as a researcher, program manager, and executive. He started as a stratospheric ozone researcher at the Goddard Space Flight Center in 1983, joined Headquarters to manage the Atmospheric Composition Modeling and Analysis Program in 1990, and was promoted to run NASA’s Earth Science research program in 1999. He served in that role till retiring.

    In that position, he managed a budget of some $500M/year while overseeing NASA’s research in all components of Earth System Science as well as enabling activities in airborne science, scientific computing, space geodesy, early career research, and education.

    For his work at NASA he received numerous awards, including the NASA-USGS Pecora Award (Individual) in 2024, the NASA Distinguished Service Medal in 2022, and three Meritorious Senior Executive awards from the Office of Personnel Management. He was elected as a Fellow by AMS and AAAS.

    An active participant in interagency and international coordination, he was NASA’s long-time principal for the US Global Change Research Program, and representative to (and chair for one year) of the Interdepartmental Committee for Meteorological Services and Supporting Research for the former Office of the Federal Coordinator for Meteorology, and was a founding co-chair of the Committee on Research and Innovation of the Interagency Council to Advance Meteorological Services. He chaired the World Meteorological Organization’s Expert Team on Satellite Systems before becoming vice chair of its Expert Team on Space Systems and Utilization.

    He has degrees in chemistry from Adelphi University (BA, 1976) and the California Institute of Technology (PhD, 1982), and did post-doctoral work at the US Naval Research Laboratory.

  2. Week 02Sep 1

    Tom Patterson

    Former Cartographer, U.S. National Park Service (Harpers Ferry Center); former President, North American Cartographic Information Society (NACIS)

    • Cartography
    • Shaded relief

    Mountains and Maps: My Career as a National Park Service Terrain Artist

    I drew my first shaded relief in 1980 as a graduate student at the University of Hawai‘i at Mānoa. It was a crude pencil rendering of Taha‘a, French Polynesia. But seeing the complex island terrain clearly represented by the interplay of light and shadows enchanted me. So started a lifelong cartographic quest.

    My talk will showcase terrain presentation on US National Park maps. The advent of digital production at the NPS launched a stylistic renaissance. We transitioned from grayscale to natural color reliefs to better represent widely diverse park environments, such as those of Canyonlands, Hawai‘i Volcanoes, and Kenai Fjords.

    3D was another sea change at the NPS. Inspired by the manual panoramas of Heinrich Berann, we employed geodata and software to mimic his exquisite work—but with greater accuracy and photo-realism. Other NPS relief presentation techniques to be discussed include texture shading, cross-blended hypsometric tints, and plan oblique relief.

    I am now involved with developing software that employs machine learning and AI for terrain presentation. My talk will conclude with a sneak peek at these amazing, and potentially disruptive, new tools.

    Biography

    Tom Patterson grew up in the Adirondack Mountains of northern New York and graduated with a B.A. in geography from SUNY Oneonta. Seeking warmer weather, he then received an M.A. in geography from the University of Hawai‘i at Mānoa.

    Tom started his career teaching classes and serving as Cartography Laboratory Manager at the University of Utah. Most of his career was spent as a cartographer for the U.S. National Park Service, Harpers Ferry Center, retiring from there in 2018.

    Presenting terrain on maps is his passion. He maintains the shadedrelief.com website, is co-developer of the Natural Earth cartographic dataset, and co-developer of the Equal Earth projection for world maps.

    Tom is a former president and executive director of the North American Cartographic Information Society (NACIS), and is active in the ICA Commission on Mountain Cartography. He lives outside of Washington, D.C. in northern Virginia.

    Artificial Societies: Bridging Geospatial Modeling and Complex Social Systems

    Geographers have long understood that human activity occurs within layers of spatial context—physical geography, infrastructure networks, and institutional systems all shape where people live, how they interact, and how societies function.

    Artificial societies bring this geographic insight to computational modeling by embedding agents not only in physical settings but also in multiple interacting institutional contexts: workplaces, political organizations, faith communities, schools, and governance structures.

    This lecture explores how artificial societies extend agent-based modeling to help geographers and geoinformation scientists model layered spatial and institutional contexts, apply scenario analysis to land use planning and regional development, and evaluate policy interventions in public health, environmental management, and urban planning—particularly when outcomes depend on compliance, coordination, trust, and governance. Understanding these trade-offs and unintended consequences is essential for evidence-based decision-making in complex geographic systems.

    Selected references

    • Clemen, Thomas, et al. “Creating artificial societies for policy decision support: a research agenda and call to action.” Simulation 101, no. 6 (2025): 645–660.
    • Tolk, Andreas, et al. “Applicability of artificial societies to evaluate health care policies.” Simulation in Healthcare 19, no. 1 (2024): 41–46.
    • Tolk, Andreas, et al. “Computational decision support for socio-technical awareness of land-use planning under complexity—a dam resilience planning case study.” Land 12, no. 5 (2023): 952.

    Biography

    Dr. Andreas Tolk is Chief Scientist for Modeling and Analysis at The MITRE Corporation (Charlottesville, VA) and adjunct faculty at Old Dominion University and George Mason University. He holds a PhD and MSc in Computer Science from the University of the Federal Armed Forces in Munich, Germany. Dr. Tolk is a Fellow of the Society for Modeling and Simulation International and senior member of ACM and IEEE.

    Dr. David Attaway

    Points, Lines, Polygons — and Now Agents

    A geospatial career, from desktop GIS to multi-agent AI

    Every geographer learns the same primitives on day one: points, lines, and polygons. This talk is the story of what happens when you carry those fundamentals forward for two decades — from digitizing features and georeferencing scanned maps in desktop GIS, to building cloud-based, multi-agent AI systems for national-security missions. The through-line is simple: GIS methods do not expire; they scale. The map is still the method — the cloud is just the new field.

    Drawing on a career that began in this very department, David walks through four real applications he has built on AWS, each rooted in a classic geospatial concept: PyroWhisper, a GenAI wildfire-spread-prediction system that turns physics-based fire models into plain-language intelligence through nine specialist agents and a human-in-the-loop commander; SAR ATR, a synthetic-aperture-radar target-recognition pipeline re-architected to run up to 88× faster; MARINER, a real-time maritime-intelligence platform tracking 50,000+ vessels on a 3D globe; and GeoWhisper, an agent that answers natural-language questions about Sentinel-2 imagery using the same NDVI, NDWI, and NBR indices taught in every remote-sensing course. The talk closes on where the field is heading — the rise of governed, production-grade agentic systems — and why spatial thinking is one of the most future-proof skills a student can own.

    Biography

    Dr. David Attaway, PhD, GISP, is a Senior Technical Account Manager (AI Specialist) on the National Security team at Amazon Web Services, where he serves as a trusted technical advisor helping mission-focused customers adopt AI/ML, generative AI, and cloud-native architectures. His career spans more than fifteen years across geospatial intelligence, data science, and cloud — from GIS analyst and subject-matter expert roles supporting intelligence agencies, through nine years at Esri as a solution engineer and technical lead on the intelligence team, to his current work architecting mission-critical AI systems at AWS.

    David is a proud graduate of George Mason University, where he earned his PhD in Earth Systems and Geoinformation Science (GGS) — the same department hosting this colloquium. His doctoral research applied temporal, spatial, and geographic analysis to the incidence of dengue fever across Kenya and Africa. He also holds a Master’s in Geographic Information Systems and an Honors B.A. in Geography (GIS Certificate) from the University of Texas at Dallas, and has been a GISP (Certified GIS Professional) since 2011.

    David’s work has earned some of AWS’s highest honors. He is a 2025 AWSome Legend Award winner — the company’s top recognition, given to roughly 120 Amazonians worldwide each year — and a 2025 and 2026 AWS TFC Legend (Platinum), a distinction awarded to just 18 of some 8,500 members of the Technical Field Community. He is the #1-ranked member of the ML/AI Technical Field Community. He has also received multiple WWPS All-Star AWSome Awards — for “Dive Deep” (Q3 2025) and “Ownership” (Q4 2024) — and, earlier in his career, Esri’s “Making a Difference” Award (2018) for leading customers to innovative AI/ML data-science workflows.

    Beyond individual delivery, David founded the NatSec GenAI Coalition of the Willing (150+ engineers) and a Space & Sensors GenAI Center of Excellence, co-instructs the School of Generative AI, and has delivered enablement to 600+ practitioners. He has authored 20+ hands-on technical blogs, published 9+ papers in accredited journals, and released 60+ open-source repositories, and he continues to serve on the Northern Virginia Community College geospatial academic advisory board. He is 6× AWS Certified. He is based in Arlington, Virginia.

    From Mason to FEMA: Federal Career Paths and Geospatial Damage Assessment

    Chris will speak on two topics. The first is practical guidance on pursuing federal employment, drawn from his own path into government service and from his experiences as a Mason student.

    The second is an in-depth look at FEMA’s Geospatial Damage Assessment (GDA) program: what a GDA is, and how it informs response leadership at FEMA, DHS, and the White House on the severity of a disaster — work that helps justify federal disaster and emergency declarations.

    Biography

    Since April 2024, Chris Hill has served as an Emergency Management Specialist (Geospatial) within the Federal Emergency Management Agency’s (FEMA) Response Geospatial Office (RGO). Throughout his seventeen years of GIS and cartographic professional experience, Chris has worked across a variety of areas—including GIS enterprise management, emergency management, response, geospatial policy and governance, developing and instructing high-level GIS trainings, serving as a liaison to high-level federal working groups, and presenting to senior stakeholders such as federal agency leaders, congressional representatives, and White House officials—to inform data-driven decision-making. He has specific expertise in leading FEMA’s Geospatial Damage Assessment (GDA) program, creating advanced geospatial and cartographic products and solutions, managing organization-wide geospatial enterprises, and developing and leading geospatial software training.

    Before arriving at FEMA in 2024, Chris worked as the Lead GIS and Nautical Charting Specialist for the Office of Navigation Systems at U.S. Coast Guard Headquarters (2019–2024), as a Cartographer for the Marine Chart Division of the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (2013–2019), a GIS Specialist contractor for SAIC (2012–2013), and a GIS/Financial Specialist for the United States Geological Survey (2010–2012).

    Chris holds a Master of Science degree in Geographic and Cartographic Sciences from George Mason University (GMU, 2012), a Graduate Certificate in Geospatial Intelligence (GMU, 2013), a Graduate Certificate in Geographic Information Systems (GMU, 2011), and a Bachelor of Science in Geography (GMU, 2010).

    Urban Heat Island Mapping: How Community Data Collection Creates Cooling Solutions

    In this talk, Morgan will discuss NOAA’s Urban Heat Island Mapping Campaign program and explain the importance of community data collection. She will also talk about her experience in public health, and how she has been able to blend her public health background into her work at NOAA.

    Biography

    Morgan Zabow is the Executive Director of the National Integrated Heat Health Information System, housed in NOAA’s Climate Program Office. In this role, she leads the NIHHIS Interagency coordination group, which brings together more than 20 federal agencies to collaborate on heat preparedness, response, and resilience. During her tenure on the NIHHIS team, she has managed and supported multiple federal heat initiatives such as the annual NIHHIS Heat Safety awareness campaign, heat tabletop exercises in more than 18 communities across the country, the creation of heat.gov, and more.

    Since 2021, Morgan has managed NOAA’s Urban Heat Island mapping campaigns, where she works with community members and organizations across the country and internationally to map the hottest parts of their communities and implement cooling solutions. In addition to heat efforts, Morgan also serves as the NOAA representative to the U.S. One Health Coordination Unit team, which addresses the intersections between human, animal, and environmental health.

    Prior to her time at NOAA, she worked for Project Horseshoe Farm, a community health non-profit in rural Alabama focused on mental health. Morgan has a Masters in Public Health in Environmental Health from Emory University’s Rollins School of Public Health, and a Bachelor’s of Science in Biology from the University of Georgia.
